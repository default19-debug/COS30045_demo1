Data story · COS30045

A bigger screen costs more to run. That is not the decision that costs you money.

Every television that can legally be sold in Australia is registered with the government, with its energy use measured under one standard test. That register is public. It contains 4,599 models currently on the market — and it shows that two televisions of the same size, sitting on the same shelf, can differ by more than $100 a year to run.

Act one

What everybody believes

Light costs electricity, and a bigger panel has more area to light.

Across all 4,599 registered models, screen area explains most of the variation in annual energy use — the correlation is 0.88, which for consumer-product data is about as tight as relationships get.

So the advice writes itself: buy a smaller television. Except that nobody shops that way. People decide roughly what size fits the room and the budget, and then choose between the models at that size. Which makes the interesting question not how much does size matter but how much room is left once size is settled.

Act two

One size. 830 choices. Nearly double the running cost.

Sixty-five inches is the most crowded size on the Australian market: 830 registered models. Every dot below is the same screen size. The only thing that varies is which one you carry home.

Taking the extremes, the gap is 6.1×: 185 kWh a year against 1,135. That is a real number but a lazy one, because it compares two products almost nobody is choosing between. The honest figure is the middle of the distribution. Eight out of ten 65-inch models fall between 360 and 652 kWh a year — at 35 cents a kilowatt hour, between $126 and $228.

A hundred dollars a year, on a television people keep for a decade. The size decision is made in the showroom; the running-cost decision is made on the spec sheet — and almost nobody reads it.

Act three

And the label on the box cannot answer the question

There is a star rating, and it works — within a screen size. Read a single row of the grid below and the stars do exactly their job: more stars, less electricity, every time. Now read a column.

A five-star 32-inch television uses a median 124 kWh a year. A five-star 86-inch television uses 988. Same five stars, eight times the electricity. This is not a defect in the rating — the Star Rating Index is deliberately calculated relative to screen size, so that a large TV can be judged against other large TVs. But it means the badge is a within-size signal being read by shoppers as an across-the-shop one.

The trap: upgrading from a five-star 55-inch to a five-star 75-inch feels like a like-for-like swap. The star count has not changed, so nothing seems to have been given up. The median five-star bill goes from $116 to $205 a year.

Act four

What it costs, at your tariff and your viewing habits

Energy figures are abstract; money is not. The chart below converts the register into dollars a year. Both assumptions are yours to change — the national average is around 35 cents a kilowatt hour, and the official label assumes 10 hours of viewing a day, which is generous for most households.

That overlap is the whole story. The bands overlap so heavily that the model you pick matters more than the size you pick — which is the opposite of how televisions are sold, and the opposite of what the star rating lets you check.

So what should you do

Three things, in order

  1. Settle the screen size first, then stop comparing across sizes. Size sets the floor on the bill and nothing you do afterwards undoes it. Once the size is chosen, the star rating becomes genuinely useful.
  2. Read the kilowatt-hour number, not the stars. It is printed on the same label, underneath the stars, and unlike the stars it means the same thing on every box in the shop. Lower is cheaper, always, with no asterisk about screen size.
  3. Multiply it by your own tariff and your own habits. The label's 10 hours a day is not your household. Use the explorer to find your exact model and put your own numbers in.

None of this requires trusting us. The register is public, the processing is documented step by step on the data and method page, and every figure on this site is recomputed from the source file rather than typed in by hand.
