About

Why this project exists

A COS30045 Data Visualisation project at Swinburne University of Technology, built on the Australian Government's public register of television energy performance.

Purpose

The one sentence this site exists to deliver

Australians comparing televisions are shown a star rating that cannot be compared across screen sizes, so the number that actually predicts the bill — kilowatt hours per year, printed on the same label — is the one they should be reading.

That is the project's Big Idea in Duarte's sense: a single complete sentence that states a point of view and says what is at stake. Everything on the Story page is built to deliver it, and anything that did not advance it was cut.

Who it is for

The primary audience is an Australian household about to buy a television — non-expert, not especially interested in energy policy, making a decision in a shop or a browser tab within the next few weeks. They are numerate enough to read a chart but have no reason to care about kilowatt hours as such. They care about dollars.

That audience dictated three things. Energy is always converted to money. The star rating is treated as the thing they already trust, so the story works by complicating it rather than dismissing it. And the call to action is a single behaviour that is free and takes five seconds: read the number under the stars.

A secondary audience — a tutor, or anyone checking the work — is served by the Data & method page rather than by cluttering the story with caveats.

What the audience should do

Compare within a screen size, not across sizes, and use the kWh/year figure to do it. The explorer exists to make that one action easy, and it points onward to the government's own product comparator for checking a specific model before purchase.

Narrative

How the story is built

The Story page follows the three-part structure from the unit: a setting the audience already accepts, a conflict that unsettles it, and a resolution that asks for one action.

Setting · Act one

Agree with the reader

Bigger screens use more power. Confirmed with every model in the register, r = 0.88. Establishes credibility before anything is challenged, and plants the detail that will matter: each vertical slice is tall.

Conflict · Act two

Show the gap they cannot see

Hold screen size constant at 65 inches. 830 models, and the middle 80% still span $126 to $228 a year. The decision they thought they had made turns out not to be the one that matters.

Conflict deepens · Act three

Remove the tool they trusted

The star rating is size-relative, so the same five stars means 124 kWh on a 32-inch and 988 on an 86-inch. The reader now has a problem and no instrument.

Resolution · Act four

Hand back a better instrument

Costs in dollars at the reader's own tariff, then three concrete steps. The kWh figure does everything the stars could not, and it is already printed on the label.

Design decisions

Why the charts look like this

Marks and channels

Quantities are carried by position on a common scale wherever possible, which is the most accurately perceived channel (Munzner). Colour is used for one categorical attribute at a time, or as a redundant encoding of a quantity that is also printed as a number. No quantity is encoded by area or angle alone, and there are no 3D charts and no pie charts anywhere on the site.

Borrowing the label's colours

The sequential ramp is the Australian Energy Rating Label's own green-to-red scale. A reader who has seen the sticker in a shop already knows which end is good, so the encoding needs no explanation — and the heat map and the cost bars agree with the product they describe.

Graphical integrity

Bars begin at zero. Axes are not truncated and no data is clipped out of a chart to tidy it up. Where a headline figure would have been flattered by the extremes — the 6.1× spread at 65 inches — the middle 80% is reported alongside it and the two outliers are labelled for what they are. Heat map cells backed by fewer than three models are left blank rather than drawn from a single product.

One honest distortion, declared

The heat map's colour ramp is spread non-linearly across its range. Energy data is strongly right-skewed, and a linear ramp painted four fifths of the grid the same green. The scale stays continuous and monotonic — higher always reads redder — and the exact value is printed in every cell, so the colour never has to be measured. This is noted here because an undeclared non-linear colour scale would be exactly the kind of thing the unit teaches you to catch.

Data-ink

Gridlines are faint and dashed, redundant axis spines are removed, values are labelled directly rather than forcing a reader to measure against an axis, and no chart carries a border, background fill or decorative element that does not encode something.

Accessibility

Every chart carries a title and a text description for screen readers. Colour is never the only carrier of meaning. The categorical palette is colour-blind safe. All controls are keyboard reachable with visible focus, touch targets meet 44 px, and animation is disabled entirely under prefers-reduced-motion.

Build

How it was made

Data processing

KNIME Analytics Platform. 29 nodes — a cleaning chain, three output branches and a profiling branch — documented on the Data & method page with row counts at every stage.

Visualisation

D3.js v7, hand-built SVG. Scales, axes, data binding with .data().join(), transitions and tooltips written from scratch — no charting library.

Front end

Hand-written HTML and CSS. No framework, no build step. System fonts only, so the site renders identically offline.
